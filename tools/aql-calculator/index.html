What is AQL?

Acceptable Quality Limit (AQL) is the core metric of the ANSI ASQ Z1 4 and ISO 2859-1 acceptance sampling systems, which are widely used by third-party inspection agencies in China. It represents the worst tolerable process average that will still allow a lot to be accepted. For example, AQL 2.5 means that when a supplier's actual defect rate is 2.5% or lower, the sampling plan will accept the lot with high probability.

Normal vs Tightened/Reduced

This calculator uses single normal inspection. Switch to tightened inspection when lot quality is unstable (stricter Ac/Re) or reduced inspection when quality is consistently good (smaller sample size).

Major vs Minor Defects

Major defects affect function, safety or regulatory compliance; Minor defects are cosmetic or usability issues that do not affect function. Major AQL is usually lower than Minor AQL.

Why On-Site Inspection Matters

AQL tells you how many to inspect and how to decide, but random sampling, testing methods and defect classification still require a trained inspector on site.

Why AQL Sampling Beats Piece‑by‑Piece Inspection

Checking a representative sample — rather than every single unit — delivers clear advantages for most quality control process:

  • Saves Time & Costs: Significantly reduces inspection overhead without compromising quality standards. Faster inspections mean direct cost savings—especially when working with third-party inspection companies that charge on a man-day basis.
  • Balances Risk & Effort: Strikes the optimal balance between catching defective products and maintaining operational efficiency.
  • Flexible Standards: Allows tailored AQL levels based on defect severity and product categories.
  • Objective Decisions: Provides clear, standardized criteria for batch acceptance or rejection.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is AQL?

AQL (Acceptable Quality Limit) is the shared language of sampling inspection. It doesn’t guarantee zero defects; it defines the maximum acceptable defect rate per batch, based on ISO 2859-1 / ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 acceptance sampling tables.

How do I choose the inspection level (I, II, III)?

Most consumer goods default to General Inspection Level II. Level I samples less (cheaper, riskier); Level III samples more, which suits high-value, high-risk or historically poor suppliers. When unsure, pick II. Want to learn more? Read our complete ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 (ISO 2859-1) AQL Sampling Inspection Guide

How do I pick AQL values like 2.5 / 4.0?

2.5 for major defects and 4.0 for minor defects is the most common combination for consumer electronics and toys. Critical defects are never allowed. If the buyer specifies values, follow the buyer.

How do I read sample size / accept / reject?

Randomly draw the sample from the batch: if the defects found are no more than the accept number, the lot passes; if they reach the reject number, the lot fails. The gap between the two maps to the double-sampling table.

Why does my supplier’s sample size differ?

Three common reasons: the supplier used Level I instead of II, a different AQL value, or double sampling instead of single. Send them this result to align the basis.

Can I copy or share the result?

Which standard does this calculator follow?

It follows the sampling tables of ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 and ISO 2859-1 at General Inspection Levels I, II and III, with AQL steps from 0.065 to 10.

What about critical defects?

Critical defects (safety, regulatory or total function failure) are not negotiated within an AQL framework — one found means the whole lot fails and a 100% inspection is required. The calculator shows this warning below the result.
